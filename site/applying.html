Applications

What a job advertisement usually contains.

An ordinary posting names an employer, the hours, the workplace, and how pay is calculated. Applications go to that employer, or through a public portal such as MyCareersFuture. This site does not receive CVs.

Updated 19 November 2026The next official sector and vacancy release is the MOM Labour Market Report for the third quarter of 2026, expected mid-December 2026.

The market behind the advertisement

The same CV meets a different market depending on the sector. In technology and finance, the industry notes show fewer vacancies and more restructuring, so advertisements tend to be specific about systems and processes. In care and public services, resident employment is still being supported, and advertisements more often mention shifts, registration, or a scheme window.

Two advertisements for similar titles can still differ on hours and workplace. The duties inside them are often shared tasks — scheduling, records, customer conversations, a named tool — even when the old job title was different.

What applications commonly include

A common application is about a page, unless the employer asks for more. It names the role, then a few tasks with some scale: how often, for whom, and what changed. It often closes with the days, hours, and start date the person can work. Photographs, NRIC numbers, passport details, bank details, and dates of birth are rarely part of that first note. Employers that want a photograph say so.

Where applications go

The employer's own careers page is the usual home of a role. MyCareersFuture, run by SWDA with GovTech, can be browsed without Singpass. Sending an application there uses Singpass and is for citizens and permanent residents. A repost on another board can point toward a role. The posting on the employer's site, or on MyCareersFuture, is the copy that matches the organisation. A role that appears in neither place is harder to trace, and the company's published address is where that trace usually starts.

Details that usually appear

  • The legal name of the employer, beyond a brand or a messaging-app room.
  • Employment or freelance, the hours, the workplace, and the pay basis: salary, hourly, or per project.
  • A closing date, where there is one, and the documents the posting asks for.
  • Cost. Ordinary employers do not charge a person to apply, to interview, or to release an offer.

Requests that sit outside ordinary hiring

  • Payment, a starter kit that has to be bought, or a cheque to bank and partly return.
  • A Singpass login, a one-time password, or an NRIC photo in the first conversation.
  • Bank details before a written offer from an employer whose name can be traced.
  • An interview that exists only inside a personal messaging app, with no company email or published phone number.

The ScamShield helpline is 1799. Reports of lost money go to the Police. ACRA's business-profile search shows whether a company name is on the register. A registration and a genuine offer are separate facts. A name that is missing from the register is a gap in that trace.

After an application goes out

People often keep a short record: employer, role, date, the arrangement in the advertisement, and where it was found. Many applications draw no interview. That pattern is especially visible in sectors MOM has described as selective. Later advertisements sometimes mention a task the earlier application left out. A third party that promises interviews in exchange for a fee sits outside the public portals.

Employment Act, CPF, and flexible-work details are published by MOM, CPF Board, and TAFEP. This note shares the general pattern.
