Applications

What a job advertisement usually contains.

A posting for full-time, part-time, hybrid, online, contract, or freelance work usually names an employer, the hours, the workplace, and how pay is calculated. You apply to that employer, or through a public portal such as MyCareersFuture. This site does not take CVs.

The market behind the advertisement

The same CV can land differently depending on the sector. In technology and finance, the industry guides show fewer vacancies and more restructuring, so advertisements tend to be specific about systems and processes. In care and public services, resident employment is still being supported, and advertisements more often mention shifts, registration, or a scheme window.

Two advertisements with similar titles can still differ on hours and workplace. The duties inside them are often familiar tasks — scheduling, records, customer conversations, a named tool — even when your previous job title was different.

What a first application usually includes

A common application is about a page, unless the employer asks for more. It names the role, then a few tasks with some sense of scale: how often, for whom, and what changed. It often closes with the days and hours you can work, and when you could start. A photograph, NRIC number, passport details, bank details, and date of birth are rarely needed at this stage. If an employer wants a photograph, the advertisement says so.

Where to send an application

The employer's own careers page is the usual home of a role. MyCareersFuture, run by SWDA with GovTech, can be browsed without Singpass. Sending an application there uses Singpass and is for citizens and permanent residents. A repost on another board can point you toward a role. The copy on the employer's site, or on MyCareersFuture, is the one that matches the organisation. If a role appears in neither place, it is harder to check. A useful next step is the company's published address.

Details that usually appear

  • The legal name of the employer, beyond a brand or a messaging-app room.
  • Employment or freelance, the hours, the workplace, and the pay basis: salary, hourly, or per project.
  • A closing date, where there is one, and the documents the posting asks for.
  • Cost. Ordinary employers do not charge a person to apply, to interview, or to release an offer.

Requests that are not part of ordinary hiring

  • A payment, a starter kit you have to buy, or a cheque you are asked to bank and partly return.
  • Your Singpass login, a one-time password, or a photo of your NRIC in the first conversation.
  • Bank details before a written offer from an employer you can identify.
  • An interview that exists only inside a personal messaging app, with no company email or published phone number.

The ScamShield helpline is 1799. If money has already been lost, that report goes to the Police. ACRA's business-profile search shows whether a company name is on the register. A company can be registered and still not be offering a real job. If the name is not on the register at all, treat that as a warning sign.

After you have applied

It helps to keep a short record: the employer, the role, the date, the arrangement in the advertisement, and where you found it. Many applications do not lead to an interview. That is especially common in sectors MOM has described as selective. A later advertisement sometimes mentions a task the earlier application left out. Anyone who promises interviews in exchange for a fee is not using the public portals.

Employment Act, CPF, and flexible-work details are published by MOM, CPF Board, and TAFEP. This page is the general pattern, not a substitute for those pages.
